# The Great War: Field Manual

This is the player handbook for The Great War. It covers how the event works from your side: your life and your nation, how wars start and end, what happens when you kill someone you're not at war with, how land changes hands, how you earn and spend money, what you can fight with, and where to get help.

It's split into five modules, the same five the Discord is split into, so if you've got a question about trade you know which channel to ask in. Section numbers match the staff copy. If a number skips, that part was staff only.

Numbers in here are the current settings. Some will move a little during playtesting, and if they do we'll say so in announcements. Read Module 1 and Module 3 before your first session, at the very least.

## Module 1: The Event and Your Life

### 1.1 The event and first principles

#### 1.1.1 The vision

Up to about 500 of you are citizens of eight rival WW1 nations on a shared, pre-built map of Europe in 1914. You start with almost nothing. Between you, you build your nation into a war machine: gathering, producing, paying tax, researching and pushing a living front line. Land only changes hands in set war windows, and every node you hold makes your nation richer. There's no official winner. The story is whatever the nations make of it.

#### 1.1.2 The platform

- Minecraft 1.20.1 on Forge 47.4.x, running Java 17.
- The event runs on its own modpack: custom-made mods built for The Great War, plus a set of performance mods.

#### 1.1.3 The event, war windows and the end

The event runs for two weeks. It's a sandbox: there's no official winner and no final score. What happens is up to the nations.

The war runs on a timetable. Every 2 days there's a **war window from 19:00 to 22:00 UK time**. Nodes can only be captured inside a window, and only then are the borders between enemies open (section 3.2.2). PvP is on all the time, though: enemies who meet on neutral land or in a contested zone can fight whenever they like. Between windows there's plenty to do (section 3.5).

#### 1.1.4 Deadlines and the web companion

Every deadline in this manual is flat real time. That covers answering an incident, the notice before an enemy marking takes effect, how long someone stays Wanted, the absent Leader handover, proposals and cooldowns. 24 h means 24 h from the moment it starts, whether the server is open or not.

That's fair because once the website is live, anything you can respond to in-game you can also do there (section 5.4). The only things that have to happen in-game are the physical ones: fighting, sieges and donating.

#### 1.1.5 First principles

These are the rules the whole event is built on. They don't bend.

1. Death costs you, but it doesn't end you. You drop what you're carrying and respawn at your capital after a wait. Lose your capital and you can't respawn until your nation takes it back (section 1.2.4).
2. No kill tickets. The game reads every kill and decides what it means (section 3.1). You never file a report to get a kill judged.
3. The game is the judge. Staff only step in to fix genuine bugs (reviving someone killed by one, for example) and for moderation. Everything else, from wars and territory to money, compensation and a nation being wiped out, is decided by the game or by the nations themselves.
4. Only the living count. Siege strength and scout intel count living players only, so nobody waiting to respawn holds a node for you.
5. The server decides. Kills, captures, payments and permissions are all settled by the server, and your screens show you what it holds.
6. Nothing is lost. Everything saves, and a restart in the middle of the event loses nothing.
7. The interface is a game, not a spreadsheet. Every system has its own screen opened by a key, laid out in the same tech tree style. The pause screen is still plain vanilla.
8. Freedom plus consequences. The game rarely stops you doing something. It makes what you do mean something instead. There are two hard blocks: allies can't damage each other, and no outcome ever depends on someone being unreachable (rule 9).
9. Nobody wins by going quiet. Nothing waits forever on someone answering. An incident nobody answers has a set outcome (3.1.2), marking a nation as an enemy needs no reply and always gives the other side at least a day's warning, and a Leader who disappears is replaced by a Regent (2.1.3).

### 1.2 Nations, membership, life and death

#### 1.2.1 The nations

There are eight nations, all historical WW1 powers: the British Empire, France, Russia, Italy, Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria. Nobody can found a new nation during the event.

Each nation has its own name, colour, capital and starting nodes. Staff pick each nation's founding leadership (the Leader and the succession order, section 2.1) before the event starts.

Each nation runs on its own server, but it's all one world to you. When you cross a border you see a short loading card for about a second, and then you're there, with your kit, wounds, money and tank.

#### 1.2.2 Membership and assignment

You belong to exactly one nation, or you're nationless (you left, you were expelled, or your nation was wiped out).

When you first join, you go into the nation with the fewest members. Ties are broken at random. You can ask for a particular nation instead, and you'll get it as long as it isn't currently the largest. If it is, you're balanced into the smallest like everyone else.

Each nation also has a cap of about 80 members (section 1.3).

#### 1.2.3 Leaving, expulsion and rejoining

- Leadership can expel you if they hold the Membership permission (section 2.1), and your Leader can expel you for killing one of your own (3.1.4). Being expelled leaves you nationless.
- If you're nationless, any nation can take you in by request (section 1.3).
- If your nation is wiped out (1.2.5), you become nationless and can join another nation.

#### 1.2.4 Death and respawning

When you die:

1. You drop your items where you died.
2. You wait out a respawn cooldown: about 2 minutes, or about 5 minutes while a war window is running.
3. You respawn at your nation's capital, or at one of its extra respawn points (below).

**If your capital has been captured, you can't respawn anywhere.** You stay dead until your nation takes it back. When it does, everyone who was waiting respawns there.

**Extra respawn points.** Research unlocks a second respawn point, and further research a third and so on (section 4.2). Your Leader chooses which nodes they are. They only work while your nation holds its capital, and you can't respawn at one that's being captured.

While you wait, whether that's the cooldown or a lost capital, you watch through the eyes of a living player of your own nation. It's first person only, and you can switch between them. There's no free camera and you can't see through walls. If there's nobody to watch, you see the death screen and the timer. You're muted in proximity voice chat until you're back.

Dying never costs a Leader the leadership or a seat holder their seat (2.1.3). Staff only revive people for genuine bugs.

#### 1.2.5 Nation lifecycle

A nation lives as long as it holds at least one node. Losing the capital doesn't end it, though nobody can respawn until it's retaken (1.2.4).

A nation is **wiped** when it loses its last node, whether to a siege (section 3.3) or to bankruptcy (section 4.3). When a nation is wiped, its members become nationless and are free to join another nation. All its wars, alliances, proposals, trades and jobs end, any money tied up in them is refunded, and nobody new can join it.

Being wiped is permanent. Staff can restore a nation, but only to correct a mistake.

#### 1.2.6 Logging out in combat

Logging out in combat counts as a death (1.2.4). You're in combat for 30 seconds after you take or deal damage, or 10 seconds after fire, a fall and the like, and it shows under your money on the HUD. Being kicked doesn't count. Timing out does.

### 1.3 Citizenship: leaving, joining, defecting

Leaving is your right. You can leave your nation at any time from Me, after a confirmation, and you become nationless. This is the check on bad leaders. A bad Leader bleeds people.

- Joining works by request. If you're nationless, you ask to join, and someone in that nation's leadership with Membership accepts or refuses.
- No defecting to the enemy. For 72 h after you leave, you can't join a nation that has your old nation marked as an enemy, or that your old nation has marked as one.
- Each nation has a cap of about 80 members, so one side can't snowball by soaking up everyone.
- If you hold a leadership seat, you lose it when you leave.

### 1.4 Player roles

Roles have been replaced by classes and specialisations. Your class decides how you fight (4.6.6) and your specialisation decides how you earn (4.6.7).

## Module 2: Leading a Nation

### 2.1 Nation governance: the Leader and the 8 seats

Think Supremacy 1914, Hearts of Iron IV and Civilization. One Leader plays the country. Leadership is the Leader plus a small cabinet, and everyone else is a citizen (section 1.3) who never has to touch the business of government unless they want to.

#### 2.1.1 The Leader

Each nation has one Leader, who holds every permission. Only the Leader can manage the seats, set the succession order and approve large treasury moves (2.1.4). Staff appoint each nation's founding Leader before the event starts.

#### 2.1.2 The 8 seats

The Leader fills up to 8 leadership seats. Each seat has a name and whatever permissions the Leader gives it. Seats start with default names, and the Leader can rename them. The name is just for show.

| Default seat name | Covers |
|---|---|
| Chancellor | Treasury and tax |
| Foreign Secretary | Mail and trade |
| War Minister | Relations and war, orders |
| Justice Minister | Incidents |
| Science Minister | Research |
| Home Secretary | Membership |
| Officer (×2) | Nothing until given permissions |

You can hold one seat at most.

This is the full list of permissions. Every leadership action falls under exactly one of them.

- **Read mail:** see the nation's diplomatic mailbox (section 2.3).
- **Write mail:** send letters (section 2.3).
- **Relations & war:** mark other nations as ally, neutral or enemy, and appoint scouts (sections 3.2 and 3.4).
- **Incidents:** forgive or demand compensation when one of your people is killed, and pay or refuse when one of yours is the killer (section 3.1).
- **Trade:** propose and answer trades (section 4.5).
- **Treasury & tax:** set tax and spend the treasury (section 4.1).
- **Research:** open and lock tech nodes (section 4.2).
- **Orders & jobs:** post rallies and jobs for citizens (section 2.5).
- **Membership:** admit and expel members (section 1.3).
- **Press:** publish articles in the Daily Front (section 2.4).

Permissions are checked live on every screen and every action. Lose a seat and you lose its screens and its mail on the spot.

#### 2.1.3 Succession and the absent Leader

The seats double as the succession order, seat 1 first. The Leader sets that order.

If the Leader leaves the nation, the first seat holder becomes Leader straight away and everyone is told. If no seats are filled, leadership passes to the longest serving member.

If the Leader has been offline for 48 h, the first seat holder who has been online in the last 24 h becomes Regent and holds the Leader's powers until the Leader comes back. The same happens if the Leader is dead and the capital is lost: the first living seat holder acts as Leader until the Leader respawns.

A seat holder who leaves or is expelled loses the seat immediately. Dying doesn't cost anyone their seat or the leadership.

#### 2.1.4 Leader-only safeguards

Any single trade, gift or spend worth more than 25% of the treasury needs the Leader's approval. It waits in the Leader's alert row until someone deals with it. A Regent can approve it too.

Only the Leader manages seats, permissions, seat names and the succession order.

#### 2.1.5 Who gets told what

Cabinet notifications go to the Leader and to the seats whose permissions cover them. Nobody else. As a citizen you only hear about things that concern you personally, your nation's orders (section 2.5), the newspaper (section 2.4) and the big public events (section 3.2.6).

### 2.2 The interface: the Leader's view and the citizen's view

Leadership plays the strategy game. Citizens play Minecraft with a purpose. Each side only sees its own view. Everything uses plain words, and every screen follows the same tech tree style.

#### 2.2.1 The citizen's view: the War Office (key P)

Press P. The War Office is a set of tiles and nothing else.

- **Me** shows your money, your nation, your class and anything waiting on you personally. It also shows the four rules:

  - Kill enemies: fine, at any time.
  - Kill someone from a neutral nation: your nation pays compensation, or you're Wanted.
  - Kill your own people: your Leader forgives you, fines you or expels you.
  - Allies can't hurt each other. Logging out in a fight is a death.

- **Orders** shows what your leadership wants right now (rallies and jobs, section 2.5) and the standing default work.
- **The Daily Front** is the newspaper (section 2.4).
- **Wanted** lists who your nation has Wanted and the bounties on them, and shows if you're Wanted yourself (section 3.1.3).
- **Map** is the war map (section 3.4).

Leave nation is inside Me, with a clear confirmation of what leaving means (section 1.3).

#### 2.2.2 The Leader's view: the Cabinet (Leader and seat holders only)

If you've played HOI4, Civ or Supremacy this will feel familiar. You only see the parts your seat's permissions cover.

- **Top bar:** treasury, tax rate, members, nodes held, wars, and the time to the next war window.
- **Alert row:** one icon per thing that needs you, each with a countdown. New mail, a trade offer, an incident waiting on your answer, a nation marking you as an enemy (and when it takes effect), an alliance or peace waiting on your side, a node under siege, a Leader approval (2.1.4) and any deadline about to run out. Click an icon and you're taken straight to the decision.
- **Nations ribbon:** every nation's flag with an icon for where you stand with them (ally, neutral or enemy, and anything waiting to take effect). Click a flag to open the country panel. It shows their Leader and seats, your relationship and its history, recent events between you, and the actions *Send letter, Propose trade, Mark as ally, Mark as neutral, Mark as enemy*.
- **Mailbox** (section 2.3): letters and trade proposals. Proposals come with their own buttons.
- **Trade** (section 4.5): the two column deal screen.
- **Incidents:** your nation's incidents both ways, and your own people's kills of each other, each with its buttons and countdown (section 3.1).
- **Government:** seats and permissions, succession order, tax, research focus, extra respawn points.
- **Citizens:** the roster (alive or waiting to respawn, seat, recent activity), orders and jobs (section 2.5) and the public treasury log.

#### 2.2.3 Interaction rules

- Any button that can't be undone or that costs money shows a one line confirmation of exactly what will happen before it goes through.
- You can always see where you stand at a glance. Your money is on the HUD, your nation is on your nameplate and in the tab list, and a badge appears when something is waiting for you.

### 2.3 Mail and proposals

Borrowed from Supremacy 1914 and Civilization.

Letters are free text messages from one nation to another, or to several at once. A seat with *Write mail* writes them, and every seat with *Read mail* on the receiving end can read them. Each letter is signed with the writer's name and seat.

Proposals are letters with a decision attached, which means trades (section 4.5). The receiver answers with buttons: **Accept**, **Counter** or **Reject**. Proposals expire after 24 h (1.1.4). Alliances and peace aren't proposals any more: each side sets them on the Nations ribbon (section 3.2).

Mail arrives in the Cabinet Mailbox and in the alert row, and unread mail gets a badge.

Each nation can send 10 letters and 10 proposals an hour. Letters max out at 1,000 characters. Mail older than 7 days gets archived. You can still read it, it just stops cluttering the inbox.

All mail is logged and staff can read it (section 5.1). Write accordingly.

### 2.4 The Daily Front

The newspaper, modelled on Supremacy's Daily European. It's the one thing everyone reads. It has its own screen in both the War Office and the Cabinet, and a short headline pops up when a new edition comes out.

A daily edition goes out every day, and breaking news runs live for the big moments.

The paper writes stories on its own for:

- nations marked as enemies, and when the war begins
- peace
- alliances formed and broken
- compensation refused, and who is Wanted
- nodes captured, capitals lost and retaken, and nations wiped out
- new Leaders
- large trades
- each nation's casualties in the last war window

A seat with *Press* can publish 1 article per day for their nation. Official statements, propaganda, threats, whatever you like. An article is a title plus up to 1,500 characters, signed with the nation and the writer.

The paper keeps the last 7 days. Staff can delete any article (section 5.1).

### 2.5 Orders and jobs

Citizens should feel like real people, not NPCs. Leadership gives you direction, and in return you get a purpose and a wage.

#### 2.5.1 Rallies

A rally is a map marker, a time and one line of text ("Defend Calais, 19:00"). You get a toast and a waypoint, and the rally stays in Orders until it expires.

#### 2.5.2 Jobs

A job is a task with a reward, paid from the treasury once the game confirms you've done it. Jobs only cover things the game can actually check:

- donate a set amount of a resource to a tech node (paid per item)
- a reward for each enemy killed, either in a given node or anywhere
- be in a node during a siege (paid per minute you're there)
- fortify a node (see below)

The budget is set aside when the job is posted, and whatever's left goes back to the treasury when the job ends.

#### 2.5.3 Fortify a node

The Leader, or a seat with *Orders & jobs*, posts a Fortify job by picking one of the nation's own nodes, a block budget and a rate of pay per block. It pays for sandbags, planks, stone, cobblestone, bricks and barbed wire placed in that node. The pay goes to whoever placed the block, but only if the block is still standing 10 minutes later. If you break your own block inside those 10 minutes, it doesn't count.

You're limited by the job's budget and by 256 paid blocks per player per day. Builders get 25% more per block (4.6.7).

The recipes for the two trench blocks:

| Recipe | Output |
|---|---|
| 4 sand + 1 string | 4 sandbags |
| 2 iron nuggets + 2 string | 4 barbed wire |

Fortified nodes are much harder to take in the next war window, so this is how a nation gets ready for one. Both count for Fortify jobs. A machine gunner can fire braced on a sandbag (4.6.13). Barbed wire slows anyone walking through it (Slowness III while you're in it) and hurts a little (half a heart every 1.5 s). Anyone means anyone, your own side included, so think about where you put it.

#### 2.5.4 Limits and pay

Every job has a total budget, and there are daily caps per job and per nation on top. Every payout goes in the public treasury log, so citizens can see exactly who's getting paid.

#### 2.5.5 Default work

When there are no orders, Orders shows the standing default: donate to the open research, gather, defend home. You're never left with nothing to do.

#### 2.5.6 Who can post

Seats with *Orders & jobs* post rallies and jobs. Spending treasury money on a job also needs *Treasury & tax*, or the Leader's approval.

## Module 3: War and Land

### 3.1 Combat and consequences

Everything else in this event hangs off this section, so read it properly. PvP is on at all times, and the game doesn't stop you hurting other players, with one exception: allies can't hurt each other. Anyone else, you can shoot. What changes is what the kill means afterwards, and that depends on who you killed and how your nations stood at the time.

#### 3.1.1 The consequence table

Every kill gets checked against this table, whatever killed them (3.1.5). What counts is the relationship at the moment of the killing blow, not what it was when the fight started.

| Who you killed | Can you hurt them? | What the kill means |
|---|---|---|
| An enemy | Yes | A legitimate act of war, in a window or not. Nothing happens to you. |
| Someone your nation has Wanted (3.1.3) | Yes | Lawful. Nothing happens to you, and you may collect a bounty. |
| Someone from a neutral nation, including one you've marked as an enemy before it takes effect (3.2.3) | Yes | An incident (3.1.2). |
| Someone in your own nation | Yes | Your Leader decides (3.1.4). |
| Someone in an allied nation | No | You can't. |
| A nationless player | Yes | No consequence. |

Damage that doesn't kill is never punished. Only kills count, so clipping a friend with splash damage won't cost you anything.

#### 3.1.2 Incidents: killing someone from a neutral nation

If you kill a player from a nation that's neither your enemy nor your ally, an incident is logged automatically. Then it goes back and forth between the two nations:

1. **The victim's nation** has 24 h to choose: **Forgive**, which closes it, or **Demand compensation**, a fixed amount. If nobody answers, it's forgiven.
2. **Your nation** then has 24 h to choose: **Pay**, out of your wallet or out of the treasury if your nation decides to cover it, or **Refuse**. If nobody answers, it counts as a refusal.
3. **Refusing makes you Wanted** by the victim's nation (3.1.3).

The compensation goes up each time the same two nations have another incident, so a rich nation can't simply pay its way through a killing spree.

On your screens these sit under Incidents in the Cabinet, and you'll get a toast when one is opened over a kill of yours.

#### 3.1.3 Wanted and bounties

If your nation refuses to pay for your kill, you're **Wanted** by the victim's nation for a few days. While you're Wanted, its players can kill you with no consequences, and they can post a bounty on you: cash for whoever kills you. Being Wanted shows on your Me tile, and your nation's own list of Wanted players and bounties is on the Wanted tile.

#### 3.1.4 Killing your own

If you kill someone in your own nation, your Leader chooses: **Forgive**, **Fine** (out of your wallet, into the treasury) or **Expel**, which leaves you nationless (1.2.3).

#### 3.1.5 What counts as your kill

You're credited with a kill however you did it:

- direct hits
- projectiles (the shooter)
- explosives and TNT you placed (the owner)
- potions and lingering clouds (the thrower)
- pets (the owner)
- fire (whoever lit it)
- artillery and tank guns

The relationship is read when the hit lands, not when you fire. A shell fired during a war that lands after peace is signed is a kill of a neutral nation's player, and opens an incident. Time your barrages. The ally block (3.1.1) covers all of these too, so you can't hurt allies with artillery or fire either.

If you find a way of killing someone that the game doesn't credit to you, report it to staff. Using it on purpose is an exploit and will be treated as one.

#### 3.1.6 Killing yourself and dying to the world

If you die to the world (falling, drowning, fire nobody started) or kill yourself, nobody answers for it, but it's still a death (section 1.2.4). If you cause someone else's "accident", say by knocking them into lava, that's your kill and the table runs as normal.

### 3.2 Relations and war

Every nation marks every other nation as an **ally**, **neutral** or an **enemy**, all from one screen: the Nations ribbon in the Cabinet (section 2.2). Neutral is where everyone starts. Nothing needs staff approval.

#### 3.2.1 Ally, neutral and enemy

| Relationship | How it's set | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Ally | Both sides must mark each other as an ally | You can't hurt each other. Allies standing in a node's rings help defend it (3.3.4). |
| Neutral | The default, and where both sides go back to for peace | Killing each other opens an incident (3.1.2). |
| Enemy | Either side can mark the other, alone | You're at war. You can fight at any time, and capture each other's nodes in the war windows (3.2.2). |

#### 3.2.2 War windows and closed borders

War windows are **every 2 days, 19:00 to 22:00 UK time**.

- **Capturing nodes** only happens inside a window (section 3.3).
- **Borders between enemies are closed between windows.** You can't cross into an enemy's land, and they can't cross into yours. Only scouts can (3.4). When a window opens the borders open. When it closes, anyone still in enemy land is sent back to their own side.
- **PvP stays on all the time.** Between windows, enemies can still meet and fight on neutral land and in contested zones (3.5).
- The borders of nations you aren't at war with stay open.

A war stays on from window to window until both sides make peace or one side is wiped out (1.2.5).

#### 3.2.3 Marking a nation as an enemy

Either side can mark the other as an enemy. It doesn't need the other side's agreement. The war starts at **the first war window at least 24 hours away**, so the other side always gets at least a day's warning. Until then, nothing has changed: you're still neutral, and a kill between you opens an incident (3.1.2).

Marking an ally as an enemy ends the alliance on the spot. The war itself still waits for the first window at least 24 hours away.

#### 3.2.4 Alliances

An alliance needs both sides. Each marks the other as an ally, and it starts the moment the second one does.

Allied players can't damage each other at all. This is the only hard block in the game.

Either side can end it at any time by marking the other as neutral or enemy. It's instant and the server is told. Any kill between you after that opens an incident unless you're at war.

#### 3.2.5 Peace

Peace needs both sides. Each sets the other back to neutral, and the war ends when the second one does. Until then the war goes on, so a losing side can't just walk off. A war also ends when either nation is wiped out.

#### 3.2.6 Notifications

Every diplomatic event pops a toast in the top right, in nation colours, and gets logged.

Everyone sees these: a nation marked as an enemy (with when the war begins), war begun, war window open and closed, peace, alliance formed, alliance broken, capital captured, capital retaken, nation wiped out, new Leader.

Only the people with the right permissions see these: incidents, demands for compensation, and payments and refusals.

If a lot fire at once they stack and get summarised, so they won't bury your screen.

#### 3.2.7 Where diplomacy lives

Leadership handles all of this in the Cabinet (section 2.2): relations are on the Nations ribbon and letters and trades come through the Mailbox.

| What | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Relations | Every nation's relationship with every other (ally, neutral, enemy), anything waiting for the other side, and when each new war begins. |
| Log | The history of diplomatic events. |

Everyone can see relations. Only permission holders can change them.

### 3.3 Nodes, cores and the ring siege

This is how land changes hands.

#### 3.3.1 Nodes

The map is split into 74 nodes drawn by staff to follow the actual geography. They aren't chunk squares.

Every node gives its owner **income** and **resources**, so some nodes are worth more than others (section 4.6.3). More nodes means more money and materials, which means better gear and more tanks.

Each node belongs to one nation and has one core: a single indestructible block, a beacon unless said otherwise. One node per nation is its capital. A few borders across water, like the Channel, are linked by staff so the nodes on either side count as neighbours.

#### 3.3.2 Front line adjacency

During a war window you can attack a node of an enemy that borders your own land. That keeps the war moving along a real front line, and the front updates as nodes change hands.

#### 3.3.3 The capture rings

Each core sits in the middle of rings that weight your capture power by how close you are:

| Ring | Weight | Reach from the core |
|---|---|---|
| Inner | 1.0 | up to 8 blocks |
| Middle | 0.5 | 8 to 16 blocks |
| Outer | 0.25 | 16 to 24 blocks |
| Beyond | nothing |  |

You only count if you're within 32 blocks above or below the core. The rings are marked in the world so you can see them. Only living players count. Creative and spectator players never do, and neither does anyone waiting to respawn.

#### 3.3.4 The tug of war

While a node is contested, the game adds up the weighted power of the living attackers (the owner's enemies that hold a bordering node) against the living defenders (the owner plus its allies). Whoever has more people present wins.

If the two are equal, the capture freezes. Any imbalance pushes the bar towards the stronger side, and the speed climbs sharply with the ratio. Roughly 14 minutes at 1.1 to 1, about a minute at 2 to 1, and about 15 seconds at 4 to 1. It can never move faster than the attackers' total presence allows, so adding a defender can never speed up a capture. If you die, log off or walk out, you stop counting immediately.

#### 3.3.5 Capturing, the capital and neutral land

Win the tug of war and the node flips to the attacker. It can be taken back, so the front keeps moving.

Capitals are fortified. Defenders count for 1.5 times as much there, which makes a capital take roughly twice as long to capture. Losing your capital doesn't end your nation, but nobody can respawn until it's retaken (1.2.4). A nation is only wiped out when it loses its last node (1.2.5).

A few edge cases:

- An undefended core still falls, at whatever pace the attackers' ring positions give them.
- If both sides leave, the bar holds for 60 seconds and then drifts back to the owner over about 3 minutes.
- A node a nation loses to bankruptcy (section 4.3) goes neutral, and anyone can claim it in a war window by standing on it. Allies pool their power. Rival nations that aren't allied block each other, and a rival has to drain the bar to zero before it can start its own claim.

### 3.4 The map

The world is **14,000 by 14,000 blocks**: Europe in 1914. On flat ground, sprint-jumping from the edge of France to the Russian border takes about 10 minutes, and real terrain makes it longer. The terrain carries on past the playable border, so the world never visibly ends.

- **Theatres.** The 74 nodes are grouped into 14 theatres on the campaign map.
- **Harsh terrain.** The Arctic, the Sahara and the Syrian desert, the Alps and the Caucasus are all built and you can go in, but they're very hard to survive. The Alps and the Caucasus can be crossed at a few passes. The Carpathians and the Pripyat marshes are infantry only: no tanks.
- **Neutral countries.** Spain, Portugal, Switzerland, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, Sweden and Persia can't be entered or captured. A community vote may change this.

Open the map from the Map tile in the War Office. You can also bind it to a key in Controls (J is free).

It shows the real terrain from above, with each node tinted in its nation's colour. Borders between nations are drawn as strong lines, and borders inside one nation as faint ones. You'll also see node names, gold stars on capitals, what each node produces, and live sieges with a contested badge and the capture bar. You can pan, zoom and centre on yourself.

What you see depends on who you are:

- Everyone sees territory, borders, names, capitals and public siege status.
- Leadership sees where their own nation's living members are, by node.
- Scouts can find out how many living enemy players are in an enemy node.

You'll never be sent a position or a count you aren't entitled to, so there's nothing for a cheat client to dig out.

**Scouts.** Each nation has a limited number of scouts, appointed by its leadership. Scouts can cross enemy borders between war windows, but they can't attack anyone from a nation they're at war with. To gather intel on a node, a scout has to stay inside it for at least 5 minutes. The count then stays visible for 30 minutes, to the scout and to his leadership. There's a cooldown before the same node can be scouted again.

### 3.5 Between war windows

Windows are only three hours every two days. The rest of the time is what decides how they go:

- **Fortify your nodes:** trenches, wire, sandbags and bunkers. A fortified node is much harder to take in the next window, and Fortify jobs pay for the work (2.5.3).
- **Work the economy:** mines, farms and oil produce most when players work them. A node's passive income is small (4.6.3).
- **Build the war machine:** tanks, ammo and gear from those resources, and research by donation (4.2).
- **Contested zones:** loot zones with prize chests, open between windows. Anyone can fight there.
- **Scouting** enemy land before the next window (3.4).
- **Orders and pay:** leaders post jobs, and you're paid for being active (2.5 and 4.6.8).

Enemies can still fight on neutral land and in contested zones between windows. Only capturing nodes and entering enemy land wait for the window.

## Module 4: Economy and Arms

### 4.1 Money, treasury and tax

#### 4.1.1 Personal money

You have one $ balance. It's whole dollars only, it carries over between sessions, and it isn't tied to your nation or to being alive. You keep your money when you die. You start with $100.

Your balance floats above your head in gold, so anyone within 32 blocks can see how rich you are. It's hidden whenever your name is hidden. It's also on your HUD, and it turns red if you go negative.

#### 4.1.2 Nation treasury

Every nation has one shared pot of money. Tax and node income fill it. Automatic costs come out of it, and so do the things leadership is allowed to spend on. If the treasury can't cover something, it doesn't happen. Section 4.3 covers what that means.

Leadership can't move treasury money into anyone's personal wallet, their own included.

#### 4.1.3 Tax

Tax is a cut of what you earn, not a charge on what you already have. It's taken the moment money lands, rounded down, and the rest is yours. You'll see a receipt on the action bar, something like "+$1,000 stall sale · 20% tax $200 to Britain · you keep $800".

Whoever holds the tax permission in your nation sets the rate, anywhere from 0 to 50%. It starts at 10%. Staff corrections are never taxed. Nationless players pay nothing. Being in debt doesn't get you off: income is still taxed.

Section 4.6.8 explains which money is taxed. Short version: money coming in from outside your nation is, pay from your own treasury isn't.

### 4.2 Production and the technology tree

This is a research tree in the style of Rise of Kingdoms. Your nation funds it by donating resources, and leadership picks which way it goes. Completed research opens up better gear tiers, new production and better pay as the event goes on.

#### 4.2.1 The tree

There's one tree and every nation uses the same one, but each nation has its own progress. Every node has a name, an icon, the node or nodes you need first, and a cost in food, iron and gold. In practice that's wheat, iron ingots and gold ingots.

The tree is fixed for the whole event. It won't be changed halfway through, so nobody's progress gets stranded.

#### 4.2.2 Donation and leadership focus

You donate food, iron and gold to an open node, straight out of your inventory. Progress counts for the whole nation. Your personal contribution to each node is recorded, so it can be rewarded later. A node completes once every cost is paid.

When a node's prerequisites are done it becomes available, but it starts locked. Whoever holds the tech permission opens the nodes your nation should research and keeps the rest locked. That's how leadership points everyone's donations down one path. You can only donate to open nodes.

### 4.3 Consequences: upkeep, bankruptcy and penalties

All of this happens automatically. Staff don't step in.

**Territory upkeep.** Every node your nation holds costs money out of the treasury every real hour. The capital costs more. More land, bigger bill. A normal nation earns more than it pays, but a badly run one won't.

**Nation bankruptcy.** A nation that can't pay its upkeep loses nodes one at a time, least valuable first, until it can. Lost nodes turn neutral. Bankruptcy alone never wipes a nation out: only losing its last node does (1.2.5).

**Personal debt.** If your balance drops below $0 you get Slowness, Weakness and faster hunger. They lift as soon as you're back to 0. Zero isn't debt, so a fresh player on $100 is never hit by this. You get into debt through fines and compensation (section 3.1) or by spending money you don't have.

### 4.4 Healing and logistics

> The Armory sells bandages and medical kits, and the Medic class heals best (section 4.6).

You have 40 health (20 hearts), and natural healing is slow: about 1 health every 16 seconds while you're fed. So field medicine is real logistics. You need medical supplies to heal, and your nation has to produce them (section 4.6.5). A nation that ignores them will win fights and still lose the war to attrition.

| Item | Heals | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Small bandage | 4 health, used up | everyone |
| Big bandage | 10 health | everyone |
| Bandage roll | 2 health a use, 15 uses | Medic |
| First aid kit | 30 health at once, plus regeneration and cures | Medic |

A Medic's bandages heal twice as much, in half the time.

### 4.5 Trade

This is lifted from Civilization's deal screen. It has two columns, *Your offer* and *Their offer*.

You can trade treasury money and resources from your nation store (food, iron and gold, collected from donations, node income and the Supply Office). You can't trade nodes. Land only changes hands through siege. A trade can be a gift too: just leave one side empty.

Nothing is held back while an offer is waiting. When it's accepted, the server checks both sides can still pay, then does the whole thing at once. If either side can't, it fails cleanly and tells you why.

Any trade over the limit in section 2.1.4 needs the Leader's approval before it can be sent. Every trade appears in both nations' public treasury logs, and the big ones make the newspaper.

### 4.6 Economy, resources and classes

This is the war economy, modelled on Supremacy 1914. Every node produces something, so every node is worth fighting over. The rule behind all of it: you earn money by playing and spend it on things that make playing better. None of this should feel like a job.

#### 4.6.1 The loop

Hold land → land gives mines, farms and oil → you mine and farm, sell to your nation, and earn pay by playing and fighting → your nation turns resources into war gear and tanks → you buy gear → you fight and take more land.

#### 4.6.2 Two kinds of material

| Kind | Examples | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Building materials | stone, cobble, dirt, sand, gravel, wood, glass, clay | Free and unlimited everywhere, same as vanilla. Building is never restricted. |
| War resources | coal, iron, gold, diamond, oil, food | Only from your nation's land, and land has limits. |

#### 4.6.3 Nodes produce resources

Every node (section 3.3) produces some mix of coal, iron, gold, diamond, oil and farm produce, each at tier 1 or tier 2. You can see it on the map.

Nodes also pay a small income on their own: every hour, each node puts money into its owner's treasury. Most of what a node is worth comes from players working it.

- **Mines** are built into the map. Their ore comes back over time (stone first, then ore), up to an allowance per session. Only the nation holding the node can mine it, or an enemy during a war window. Everything else inside a mine can't be broken.
- **Farms:** crops only grow up to the node's limit, and farmland in a farm node grows faster and harvests more. Research can raise the limit.
- **Oil:** an oil derrick placed in an oil node fills barrels. Barrels and fuel cans refuel tanks (4.7.3).

Wood and building blocks are unlimited (section 4.6.2).

Villager trading, iron from iron golems and piglin bartering are all off, along with any other automatic source of war resources.

#### 4.6.4 The Supply Office and the Armory

Every capital has two counters, a Supply Office and an Armory. Open either one and you get a simple screen like the ones in section 2.2.

At the Supply Office you hand in iron, coal, gold and food. You're paid in $ from the treasury at your nation's buying prices, and the items go into the nation store.

At the Armory you spend $ on war gear: guns, ammo, armour, melee weapons, grenades and medical kits. The money goes into the treasury. The Armory only stocks gear your nation has researched and produced, and it only offers you gear your class can use (section 4.6.6).

Prices are set by whoever holds *Treasury & tax*, inside the limits staff have set for each item. Every price change and every purchase goes in the public treasury log.

You can't craft guns, ammo or attachments. Your nation's armour, and a few melee weapons, can be crafted once your nation has researched them. `/recipes` opens a recipe book showing everything you can craft or buy, what it costs and what research it needs.

#### 4.6.5 Production

Nobody stands at a factory by hand. A seat with *Research*, or the Leader, queues production orders like "200 rifle rounds", "20 Lee-Enfields" or "50 medical kits". Each order costs resources from the store, money from the treasury and some real time. When it's done, the goods go into the Armory's stock.

Research (section 4.2) sets what your nation can produce. Lose your coal nodes and your factories stall.

#### 4.6.6 Weapon classes

Every gun in the event is a WW1 weapon, from the event's custom-made mods. Every gun has a class, a research tier and a price.

You pick one of five classes, and your class controls which guns you can buy and fire.

| Class | Weapons | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Rifleman | bolt-action, lever and single-shot rifles, carbines, bayonets | The backbone |
| Marksman | all Rifleman rifles, plus scopes (Marksman only) and the Sharps | Long range |
| Assault | shotguns, then SMGs late in the war | Trenches and close quarters |
| Machine Gunner | Madsen LMG, then the MG 14/17 | Holds the line |
| Medic | pistols and the full medical kit | Keeps people alive; heals far better |

Pistols and revolvers are open to every class. So are small and big bandages.

Tier 1 weapons are there from day one. Tiers 2 and 3 have to be researched (section 4.2). Each nation's Armory sells its own historical rifle at a discount: the SMLE, Gew 98, Lebel, Mosin and so on.

You can carry another class's gun. You can't fire it.

Class caps are per nation. You can have one Machine Gunner for every 8 members, and the same for Marksmen. Switching class costs $500 and then you wait 24 h before you can switch again.

You get one free basic kit for your class. It's marked *issued*, and the Supply Office won't buy it back.

Every other source of weapons is blocked: modern guns, the weapons dealer villager and its loot, and anything else that isn't on the event's weapon list.

#### 4.6.7 Specialisations

Your class is how you fight. Your specialisation is how you earn. It's optional and you can only have one.

| Specialisation | Buff |
|---|---|
| Miner | 25% more ore from every ore you mine |
| Farmer | 25% bigger harvests |
| Builder | Builds and breaks blocks faster, and gets 25% more pay on building jobs. The *Fortify a node* job (section 2.5) pays a Builder 25% more per block, out of the job's budget. Fortifications are the Builder's work; there's no Sapper class. |

Buffs raise how much you produce, never what you can sell it for, so you can't pass them on to your mates. Switching specialisation costs $500, with a 24 h wait before you can switch again. You start with none.

#### 4.6.8 Pay

You get paid for playing.

Service pay is a small amount for every minute you're online, alive and actually doing something, up to a daily cap. Stand still and do nothing for five minutes and it stops. It comes out of your nation's treasury. The rate depends on your rank (section 4.6.9), and seat holders and the Leader get a bonus for running the country. Staff set the rates, so leaders can't vote themselves a pay rise.

Combat pay comes from the treasury for enemy kills, for capturing a node and for defending one.

If the treasury can't cover pay, you don't get paid that time. The treasury never goes negative to pay you. You'll see "Britain couldn't pay you today" and your Leader gets an alert.

Tax (section 4.1) only applies to money coming in from outside your nation: stall sales, bounties and trades. It never touches pay or Supply Office payments, since both already come out of your own treasury.

#### 4.6.9 Rank, medals and your record

You earn XP by fighting (enemy kills, captures and defences), by supplying (Supply Office sales, capped per day) and by staying alive.

The ranks are Private → Corporal → Sergeant → Lieutenant → Captain. Each step raises your service pay and gives a small perk, like a discount at the Armory. Your rank shows next to your name.

Medals are for the moments people talk about afterwards: first into a captured node, holding a node while outnumbered, 10 kills in one battle, longest survivor and so on. They show on your profile, and the best ones make the Daily Front.

Your record tracks kills, captures, money earned and days survived.

#### 4.6.10 Player stalls

You can put down a stall, which is a shop chest, on your own nation's land. Sell anything you own from it (food, blocks, gear) for $, to anyone who walks up. Sales are taxed (section 4.6.8). Buying is instant, and it either goes through completely or not at all.

#### 4.6.11 Property protection

| Where | Who can break and place blocks and open containers |
|---|---|
| Your nation's land | Only your nation, plus staff |
| An enemy's land, inside a war window | The enemy too (raids and sieges). Between windows they can't even get in (3.2.2). |
| An ally's land | Never you. Allies are always protected from each other. |
| Neutral land | Anyone |

You can also lock a personal chest to yourself, so your own countrymen can't help themselves to it.

#### 4.6.13 Health, damage and armour

You have **40 health** (20 hearts). Guns hit by their class, not by their model, and no single gun hit can kill you from full health.

**Guns.** Damage is against an unarmoured player. Past its full-damage range a gun hits for less.

| Gun class | Body | Head | Full damage up to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bolt-action and single-shot rifles (the Sharps included) | 26 | 32 | 150 m |
| Semi-automatic rifles and the Winchester 1873 | 18 | 24 | 100 m |
| Pistols, revolvers, the revolver carbine and the Pedersen Device | 10 | 14 | 30 m |
| Submachine guns | 7 | 10 | 20 m |
| Shotguns (one blast is one hit) | 30 | 30 | 10 m |
| Shotgun slugs | 24 | 30 | 30 m |
| Machine guns | 8 | 11 | 100 m |

Two rules always hold:

1. **No one-shots.** A gun hit on a player at full health always leaves them at least 2 health, headshots included.
2. **Two rifle hits kill** a player in a uniform, body or head. Through the heaviest body armour it takes three body hits.

A few more rules:

- A gun takes a moment to raise after you sprint or switch to it: 1 s, or 0.6 s for pistols and SMGs. You can't fire while sprinting.
- Rifles and machine guns are badly inaccurate from the hip. Aim down the sights to hit anything past a few blocks.
- **Machine guns fire prone only:** lying down (the crawl key), or braced, which means sneaking with a sandbag just in front of you. You can't sprint with one in your hands, and it's heavy.

**Melee** plays like vanilla: the same attack cooldown, critical hits while falling, and sweeps with blades. Vanilla's swords, shield and trident are gone. These replace them:

| Weapon | Damage |
|---|---|
| Rifle butt (no bayonet) | 3 |
| Trench knife | 5 |
| Bayonet, held or fitted to a rifle | 6 |
| Cavalry sabre | 7 |
| Entrenching tool (also digs like a shovel) | 9 |
| Trench club | 9 |
| Trench shield | blocks 12 blows, then needs repair at an anvil |

**Armour.** Every piece you wear takes a flat share off all the damage you take: bullets, melee, explosions and tank fire. Where the hit lands doesn't matter. The shares add up, but **never past 30%**. Falls, fire, drowning and starving aren't reduced.

| Piece | Reduction | Speed | Lasts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cap or hat | 2% | | never wears out |
| Steel helmet | 8% | | 40 hits |
| Tunic, trousers or boots | 2% each | | never wear out |
| Body armour tier 1: padded vest | 6% | | 30 hits |
| Body armour tier 2: steel body shield | 10% | 5% slower | 45 hits |
| Body armour tier 3: heavy trench armour | 14% | 10% slower, sprint 3 s then rest 3 s | 60 hits |

- Body armour is worn over your uniform in the chest slot. Its reduction replaces the tunic's, and your tunic still shows underneath so your nation reads.
- Each tier of body armour is made from the tier below, at a crafting table or an anvil, once your nation has researched it.
- Each level of Protection on a piece adds 1%, still under the 30% cap.
- Armour wears out one hit at a time. A piece gives full protection right up until it breaks. Mend helmets and body armour at an anvil, or buy new ones at the Armory.
- Armour-piercing ammo (FMJ and slugs) ignores half the protection.
- You can carry another nation's armour, but you can't wear it. Nationless players can only wear leather.
- Your class changes your speed in uniform: Marksmen and Medics move 5% faster, Machine Gunners 5% slower. Body armour's weight comes on top.

A full kit (helmet plus heavy trench armour) takes 26% off.

### 4.7 Tanks

There are four WW1 tank designs, one for each nation that actually fielded one.

| Nation | Tank | Health | Speed | Seats | Weapons | Alive at once |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| British Empire | Mark V | 600 | about 2.5 blocks/s | driver, 2 side gunners, front machine gunner | two 6-pounders (one per side), 1 MG | 2 |
| Germany | A7V | 650 | about 3 blocks/s | commander and driver, main gunner, 2 MG gunners | 57 mm in the nose, 2 MGs | 2 |
| Italy | Fiat 2000 | 650 | about 2.5 blocks/s | driver, turret gunner, 2 MG gunners | 65 mm turret gun, 2 MGs | 2 |
| France | Renault FT | 360 | about 5 blocks/s | driver, turret gunner | 37 mm turret gun | 3 |

The other four nations build a licensed copy of someone else's design, at 20% more in resources, money and build time. Russia can build the Mark V or the FT, and Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria build the A7V.

#### 4.7.1 Getting one

The Armour research node (after Industrial War, tier 3) lets your nation build its tank. A seat with *Relations & war*, or the Leader, orders one from the Motor Pool section of the Cabinet. It costs iron, gold and coal from the nation store, money from the treasury, and then real time: about 6 h for a heavy tank, 3 h for the FT.

The finished tank rolls out next to the Motor Pool block in your capital.

A nation can have two heavy tanks at most, counting the ones still being built, or three FTs. The whole server is capped at 12. A destroyed tank is gone for good and has to be built again.

#### 4.7.2 Crew

Only living members of the owning nation can get in, so nobody can steal your tank. Use to get in, Sneak to get out, Left Alt to change seat.

The Motor Pool section lists every tank with its health, where it is and who's aboard. From there you can assign a crew, so only they can get in, or recall a tank, which kicks everyone out.

The driver drives: W and S, with A and D turning on the spot. The driver can't shoot. Each gunner has their own seat, view and gun. The gun follows where you look, within its arc, and fires on Attack. The HUD shows the arc under your crosshair and where the shot will land. The crew are safe inside. Anything aimed at them hits the hull instead.

#### 4.7.3 Moving

Tanks are slow. They climb one block steps, not two. In water they slowly sink, and deep water finishes them. They can't go into the Carpathians or the Pripyat marshes (3.4). A tank won't drive into land its driver can't build on, which means an ally's land or the land of a nation you aren't at war with. It can always drive out of land it's already in. An empty tank comes to a stop after 30 s.

Tanks run on fuel, and the driver sees the gauge. A full tank lasts about 15 to 20 minutes of driving. An empty one can't move, but its guns still fire. Refuel from outside with an oil barrel or a fuel can, or stoke coal from the driver's seat. You can also buy fuel at the Motor Pool, but it's expensive.

#### 4.7.4 Fighting

Tank cannons explode with splash damage that hurts both people and tanks. Tank machine guns hit people the same as an infantry machine gun and follow the armour rules in section 4.6.13. No tank weapon ever breaks blocks.

Against a tank, rifles do almost nothing. Armour-piercing (FMJ) rounds do a little and machine guns a bit more. Another tank's cannon does full damage. The Bundle Charge (Assault, tier 2, from the Armory) can be thrown or stuck on a tank by hand, and it goes off after 3 s for heavy damage.

You can't damage your own nation's tanks or an ally's. The dead can't damage any tank.

#### 4.7.5 Repair

A crew member with a repair kit (crafted from iron) can slowly fix a tank once it's gone 30 s without being hit. A Builder repairs 50% faster.

#### 4.7.6 Losing one

At zero health a tank blows up. The blast doesn't break blocks, but every crew member takes heavy damage, enough to kill anyone already hurt. The kill goes to whoever destroyed the tank.

When a node is captured, any empty tanks of the losing nation sitting in it are destroyed. When a nation is wiped out, all its tanks go with it.

Every tank built and destroyed, and who destroyed it, goes in the nation's log and the Daily Front.

## Module 5: Screens, Website and Getting Help

### 5.1 Getting help from staff

Staff don't referee ordinary outcomes (1.1.5, rule 3). If you lost a fight fair and square, that's the war. What staff do handle:

- **Genuine bugs.** Staff can fix things a bug broke, including reviving someone who died to one. Dying to your own bad decisions isn't a bug.
- **Exploits.** A way of killing someone that the game doesn't credit, or any other hole in the rules (3.1.5).
- **Moderation.** Staff can read any nation's mail, remove newspaper articles, and mute a nation's mail or press for a while.

Every staff action is logged with the name of whoever took it.

### 5.4 The web companion

> Coming later. Everything in game works without it.

The web companion is a website that mirrors your in-game screens, so you can see what's going on and act on it without being on the server.

You sign in with an account linked to your Minecraft account, and you see exactly what your in-game screens would show you. Same view, same permissions.

Anything leadership does from a screen can be done from the website:

- answering incidents: forgive, demand compensation, pay or refuse
- marking nations as ally, neutral or enemy
- letters and trades
- seat and succession management
- tax, research focus, orders and jobs

Nothing physical happens on the website. Fighting, sieges and donating items to the research tree all stay in game.

Toasts (3.2.6) will reach you off the server too, on the website and possibly as a Discord DM.

### 5.5 The intro, title screen and pause screen

The title screen is ours. It has one "Join the war" button and your public event profile: nation, rank, class, specialisation, medals and record. If the server's offline it shows the last copy it had. It never shows your money or anything private.

The pause screen is plain vanilla, no extra buttons. Our screens open by key: War Office on P, Cabinet on I. Minecraft's Social Interactions has been moved off P so nothing clashes.

Minecraft's own screens are left alone on purpose, so you aren't hunting for things. Options, the death screen, disconnects, loading screens, chat, tooltips and the pause screen all look and work the way you already know. Ours are the intro, the title screen, the failed join screen, and our game screens: War Office, Cabinet, Supply Office and Armory, the map and the research tree. The game window is called The Great War.

#### 5.5.1 The intro and the menu background

The intro plays once each time you start the game. Skip is in the bottom right, or press Space, Enter or Esc. "Watch intro" on the menu plays it again.

The bunker ambience and the intro booms play on the master volume, not the music slider, so turning music off won't silence them. Nothing else plays in the menus.

Behind the menu there's a looping video of a dark bunker. It's built into the pack, so there's nothing extra to install, and it works the same on Windows and macOS.

Under "Join the war" there's a status line that updates every few seconds, like "Online · 3 at war · 42 ms", or "Can't reach the front right now · retrying" if the server's down.

If a join fails before you reach the world, you get one plain sentence and a "Back to the menu" button. If anything in your pack doesn't match the event's, it's listed by name so you know what to remove or reinstall. If you're not on the whitelist you'll see "This front is closed to you for now." Once you've been in the world, a disconnect shows Minecraft's normal screen.

### 5.6 Worked examples

Some examples of how things play out. Britain and Germany are neutral unless it says otherwise.

#### 5.6.1 Marking an enemy

Say the next war windows are Monday and Wednesday at 19:00. On Monday at 15:00, Germany marks Britain as an enemy. Monday's window is only 4 hours away, so the war begins at Wednesday's window, the first one at least 24 hours off. Britain gets the toast straight away and spends two days digging in.

#### 5.6.2 Between windows

Britain and Germany are at war. On the Thursday, between windows, Bob (Britain) tries to walk into Germany and is stopped at the border. He meets Hans (Germany) in a contested zone instead, and Hans shoots him dead. That's an ordinary act of war. Bob drops his kit and respawns in London after about 2 minutes.

#### 5.6.3 Killing a countryman

Bob kills a fellow Briton over a chest. Britain's Leader chooses Fine, and the money comes out of Bob's wallet and goes into the treasury.

#### 5.6.4 The capital falls

In a war window Germany captures London. Every Briton who dies from then on stays dead, watching through the eyes of the Britons still alive. In the next window Britain's survivors and France, its ally, retake London, and everyone who was waiting respawns there at once.

#### 5.6.5 Wiped out

Germany takes Britain's last node. Britain is wiped out. Every Briton becomes nationless, its alliance with France ends, and Bob joins France.

#### 5.6.6 Logging out in a fight

Bob takes a bullet, panics and quits the game ten seconds later. He was still in combat, so it counts as a death: his kit drops where he stood, and he respawns in London after the usual wait.

#### 5.6.7 Peace

After three windows Britain and Germany are both worn out. Britain sets Germany back to neutral. Nothing changes until Germany does the same, and then the war is over.

#### 5.6.8 Switching sides

Italy is allied to Germany. In a window, Italy marks Germany as an enemy. The alliance breaks on the spot and the whole server sees it, but Italy's war on Germany only starts at the first window at least 24 hours away. Until then, any kill between Italians and Germans opens an incident.

#### 5.6.9 An incident refused

Hans (Germany) kills Marco, an Italian, while Germany and Italy are neutral. An incident opens. Italy demands compensation, and Germany refuses. Hans is now Wanted by Italy for a few days: any Italian can kill him with no consequences, and an Italian puts a bounty on his head. If it happens again between the two nations, the compensation is higher.
